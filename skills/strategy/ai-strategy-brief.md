# AI Strategy Brief

An AI strategy brief is not a roadmap. It is not a vision document. It is not a list of initiatives.

It is a one-page record of three things: what you are doing, what you have decided not to do, and what you are deferring — and the reasoning behind each. The discipline of writing it is the strategy. If you cannot fill in all three columns with specific entries, you do not yet have a strategy — you have a posture.

The brief is an internal document. Its audience is your direct leadership team. Its function is alignment: after reading it, your team should be able to make AI decisions in your absence that you would have made yourself.

---

## Step 1: The Three-Column Inventory

Before any drafting, complete this inventory. Be specific. If an entry is vague, push it to "deferring" until you have enough clarity to place it.

### Column 1: Doing

AI initiatives, deployments, or investments that are active — with budget, ownership, and a definition of what success looks like.

For each entry, capture:

- What it is (one sentence, operational not aspirational)
- Who owns it (a name, not a function)
- What success looks like at 6 and 12 months
- The primary risk if it doesn't work

*Test*: Could a new member of your leadership team read this and make a meaningful resourcing decision based on it? If not, it's not specific enough.

### Column 2: Not Doing

AI capabilities, initiatives, or approaches that you have evaluated and explicitly decided against — for now.

This column is the most important and most often omitted. Every "not doing" entry is a decision you've made and a future question you've answered in advance. It frees your team from relitigating closed questions every time a vendor calls or a trend piece circulates.

For each entry, capture:

- What it is
- Why you decided against it (specific reasoning, not generic caution)
- What would change the decision

*Test*: Is this a genuine decision, or just something you haven't got around to? If the answer is the latter, it belongs in Column 3.

### Column 3: Deferring

AI capabilities or decisions that are not ruled out but are not active — because you lack the data, the conditions, or the organisational readiness to commit.

For each entry, capture:

- What it is
- What is blocking a decision (specific, not "it's complex")
- What signal or condition would move it to Column 1 or Column 2
- The expected timing for the next review

*Test*: Every item in this column should have a trigger — something that would move it. If it has no trigger, it is either a decision you haven't made (Column 2 with more work) or a genuine deferral you can date.

---

## Step 2: The Rationale Layer

Once the inventory is complete, add one paragraph of rationale that ties the three columns together into a coherent position.

This paragraph answers: *Why is this the right AI stance for our organisation at this moment?*

It should reference:

- The specific characteristics of your organisation that shape your AI position (data maturity, talent availability, regulatory context, competitive environment, capital constraints)
- The core bet you're making — what you believe about how AI will or won't create value in your sector over the next 2-3 years
- The primary risk you are managing against

If you cannot write this paragraph in 100 words or fewer, the strategy is not yet coherent. The paragraph is not a summary of the columns — it is the reasoning that makes the columns make sense together.

---

## Step 3: The Decision Framework

Add a short decision framework: three to five questions that a member of your leadership team should ask before committing to any new AI initiative.

These questions are not bureaucratic gates. They are heuristics that reflect your actual reasoning — the questions you would ask if you were in the room.

Examples (adapt to your actual reasoning):

- Does this initiative require data we already have, or data we would need to create?
- Is there a named owner outside the technology function who will be accountable for outcomes?
- What is the adoption surface — how many people need to change their behaviour, and have we planned for that?
- Does this create vendor dependency in a capability we consider strategic?
- What is the exit path if this doesn't work at six months?

The right questions are the ones that would have caught your past mistakes, and that reflect the specific failure modes you've seen in your industry.

---

## Step 4: Format and Distribution

**Format**: One page. Deliberately. If it runs to two pages, the thinking is not yet clear enough. Cut until it fits.

**Sections**:

1. Our AI Position (the rationale paragraph — 100 words max)
2. Active Investments (Column 1 — each entry 2-3 lines)
3. Explicit Decisions Against (Column 2 — each entry 2-3 lines)
4. Deferred Decisions (Column 3 — each entry 2-3 lines)
5. Our Decision Framework (the questions — bullets)

**Distribution**: Direct leadership team only, in the first instance. This document will surface disagreements. It is better to surface them with your direct team before the document circulates more widely.

**Cadence**: Revisit quarterly. The brief is not a planning document — it does not need to survive a full year. It needs to be accurate for the next 90 days.

## Principles

**The brief is the forcing function.** Most AI strategy conversations are circular because nothing is written down. Writing the brief forces precision. The moment you try to write "not doing" entries and find them empty, you know you have not made the decisions you thought you had.

**"Not doing" is strategy.** The executive who can say "we evaluated X and decided against it for these reasons" is demonstrating more strategic clarity than the executive who says "we're exploring X." Explicit decisions against preserve optionality in a way that drift does not.

**Alignment is the output.** The brief is not for external audiences. It exists so that your direct team can make decisions in your absence that you would have made yourself. If the brief is too vague for that, it has not done its job.

**The strategy follows the brief, not the other way around.** Many executives try to write a strategy and then summarise it into a brief. The better approach is the reverse: write the brief first, to force clarity, and let the more detailed strategy follow from the clarity the brief produces.
