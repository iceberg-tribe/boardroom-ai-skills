# Board Narrative

A board narrative on AI is not a strategy presentation. It is an account of where you are, how you got here, and what you're watching for — told with enough honesty to be credible and enough structure to be useful.

Most AI board narratives fail in one of two ways: they overclaim (presenting ambition as progress) or they underclaim (hedging so thoroughly that the board loses confidence in executive judgment). This skill helps you find the register between them.

---

## Step 1: Establish Ground Truth

Before any narrative, you need an honest internal read. Answer these questions for yourself — not for the board.

**What is actually happening?**

- What AI tools or systems are live in your organisation right now?
- What pilots are underway, and what have they produced?
- What have you stopped or de-prioritised, and why?

**What have you decided?**

- What have you explicitly decided to do on AI — and when?
- What have you explicitly decided not to do — at least for now?
- What are you deferring, and what would change the deferral?

**What don't you know?**

- What are the two or three things that would most change your AI position if you understood them better?
- Where are you operating on assumption rather than evidence?

Be precise here. Vague answers produce vague narratives. A board that asks a hard follow-up question and gets a vague answer loses more confidence than a board that hears "we don't have clear evidence on that yet."

---

## Step 2: Identify Your Narrative Posture

Based on your ground truth, identify which narrative posture fits your actual situation. Choose one — the narrative fails if it tries to be more than one.

### Posture A: Deliberate Progression

*For executives who are moving methodically, have completed early learning phases, and are now making staged bets.*

The story: We have learned what we needed to learn. We know what we're building toward and why. Here is our sequence and our rationale.

Works when: You have genuine completed pilots, a clear next phase, and a rationale you can defend.
Fails when: The "progression" is actually lateral movement dressed as forward progress.

### Posture B: Informed Caution

*For executives who have evaluated the landscape seriously and made a considered case for not moving faster.*

The story: We have looked hard at this. Here is what we found. Here is why our current pace is a decision, not a default. Here is what would change it.

Works when: You can show the work — that caution is the output of analysis, not the absence of it.
Fails when: Caution is actually avoidance, and the board can sense it.

### Posture C: Active Reorientation

*For executives who have concluded that their previous AI position was wrong and are actively changing course.*

The story: What we tried didn't produce what we expected. Here is what we learned. Here is what we're doing differently and why it's more likely to work.

Works when: You can describe what you learned from the failure with specificity.
Fails when: The reorientation is framed as someone else's fault, or the new direction is as vague as the old one.

### Posture D: Foundation Building

*For executives whose AI adoption is blocked by underlying data, talent, or technology debt — and who are addressing that debt first.*

The story: Sustainable AI adoption requires foundations we don't yet have. We are building them. Here is the connection between what we're doing now and what it enables.

Works when: The foundation work is real and the connection to AI capability is credible.
Fails when: Foundation building is a deferral dressed as a strategy.

## Step 3: Structure the Narrative

A board AI narrative has five components. Keep each one short.

### 1. The Situation (2-3 sentences)

Where is your industry and your organisation in the AI moment? Anchor to something specific — a market signal, a competitive development, a regulatory change — that your board will recognise. Do not open with hype and do not open with defensiveness.

### 2. What We've Done (3-5 sentences)

What you've actually done — deployments, pilots, decisions, learning investments. Specific over general. "We piloted X in Y function, which produced Z outcome" is better than "we've been exploring AI across multiple functions." If you've made decisions to stop or de-prioritise, say so. That signals judgment.

### 3. What We've Learned (2-4 sentences)

The most credible part of any AI narrative is what you've learned — because it signals that you're engaging with reality rather than performing readiness. Name one or two specific things that surprised you or changed your view.

### 4. What We're Doing Next (3-5 sentences)

Your near-term direction. Tied to a rationale. With rough timing. Not a roadmap — a direction with enough specificity that the board can hold you accountable to it.

### 5. What We're Watching (2-3 sentences)

The two or three signals that would change your position — that would cause you to accelerate, slow down, or change direction. This is the most sophisticated part of the narrative, and the most credible: it shows that your current position is not fixed dogma but a calibrated stance under uncertainty.

---

## Step 4: Pressure-Test the Narrative

Before presenting, stress-test against the three most common board AI questions.

**"Are we moving fast enough?"**
Your narrative should give you a grounded answer — not a reassurance. If you're moving deliberately, explain why the pace is a decision. If you're behind where you want to be, say so and explain what's slowing you down.

**"What are our competitors doing?"**
You should know, specifically. If you don't, say you're monitoring it and describe how. A board that knows you haven't looked is not reassured by confident-sounding assertions.

**"What's the risk of getting this wrong?"**
Name the actual risks — not generic AI risks, but the specific risks in your sector and your organisation. Overinvestment in the wrong capability. Underinvestment that creates talent attrition. Regulatory exposure. Vendor lock-in. The board that hears specific risks trusts the executive more than the board that hears "we're managing risk appropriately."

## Principles

**Credibility is the primary asset.** An AI narrative that overclaims and is later contradicted by events costs more than a cautious narrative that proves accurate. Board trust is built on calibration, not optimism.

**Specificity is the signal.** Boards have heard many AI presentations. The ones that stay with them are specific: a specific outcome, a specific learning, a specific signal they're watching. Generalities register as the absence of engagement.

**Uncertainty is not weakness.** "We don't yet know" is a credible answer if it's followed by "and here's how we're finding out." It is a weak answer if it's the end of the sentence.

**The narrative is not the strategy.** The board narrative communicates your position. The strategy is the work behind it. If the strategy is thin, the narrative will not hold under questioning. Build the strategy first; the narrative follows.
