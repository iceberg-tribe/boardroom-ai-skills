# Name the Pressure

Before you can decide what to do about AI adoption pressure, you need to know what kind of pressure you're actually facing. Most executives are responding to several distinct pressures simultaneously — as if they were one thing. They are not. Each has a different source, a different urgency, and a different appropriate response.

This skill helps you name each pressure precisely, so you can respond to the right thing.

---

## Step 1: Inventory the Pressure

Ask the executive to describe, without filtering, what they're hearing about AI — from whom, in what contexts, and with what stated or implied urgency.

Prompts to draw this out:
- What has your board said or asked about AI in the last two board cycles?
- What are you hearing from your direct reports about AI?
- What are competitors, peers, or industry bodies saying that you're aware of?
- What have your existing technology vendors told you about their AI direction?
- What are your high-performers saying — are any of them making noise about wanting AI tools?

Capture the inventory without categorising yet. The full picture first.

---

## Step 2: Categorise Each Pressure

For each item in the inventory, identify which type of pressure it represents. A single situation may contain multiple types.

### Type 1: Board Mandate
*What it sounds like*: "We need an AI strategy." "Are we doing enough?" "What's our position on generative AI?"
*What it actually requires*: A coherent narrative, not necessarily a new initiative. Boards asking about AI usually want evidence of thoughtful engagement, not proof of deployment.
*The risk of misreading it*: Executives who interpret board interest as a deployment mandate initiate projects before they have a rationale. This creates Pilot Trap risk.

### Type 2: Competitive Signal
*What it sounds like*: "[Competitor] just announced an AI partnership." "The industry is moving." "We'll be left behind."
*What it actually requires*: Signal analysis, not response. Most competitive AI announcements are themselves AI theatre — press releases ahead of operational reality. The question is whether the signal reflects genuine capability or performed readiness.
*The risk of misreading it*: Reactive deployment to match a competitor's announcement that doesn't reflect their actual operations.

### Type 3: Internal Fracture
*What it sounds like*: "Our tech team wants to build AI tools." "Some teams are already using ChatGPT." "There's a divide between those who think we should move fast and those who think we should be careful."
*What it actually requires*: Governance clarity. The pressure here is not about AI adoption; it is about internal alignment on who decides what, within what guardrails.
*The risk of misreading it*: Treating a governance problem as a strategy problem — publishing an AI strategy when what you need is a decision framework.

### Type 4: Vendor Migration
*What it sounds like*: "Our [ERP / CRM / analytics vendor] is pivoting everything to AI." "They're deprecating the old interface." "We're being pushed to their AI-first product."
*What it actually requires*: Vendor assessment, not AI strategy. This is a procurement and dependency risk question, not a capability question.
*The risk of misreading it*: Framing a vendor migration decision as an AI strategy decision — and over-investing strategically in what is actually a contract negotiation.

### Type 5: Talent Signal
*What it sounds like*: "Our best people are asking about AI tools." "We're losing candidates to companies that have better AI capabilities." "People are frustrated that we're not moving faster."
*What it actually requires*: A specific, credible answer to "what are we doing and when will it matter to you?" Talent pressure is about agency and relevance, not strategy.
*The risk of misreading it*: Publishing a strategy that doesn't translate into tool access for the people asking.

### Type 6: Regulatory or Compliance Pressure
*What it sounds like*: "We need to be ready for the EU AI Act." "Our regulator has asked about our AI governance." "Clients are asking about our AI risk controls."
*What it actually requires*: Governance and documentation, not capability development. This is a legal and compliance framing question.
*The risk of misreading it*: Conflating compliance readiness with AI strategy — over-investing in governance for AI you don't have, or under-investing in governance for AI you do.

---

## Step 3: Map the Pressure Landscape

Once each pressure is categorised, produce a map:

**High urgency / High stakes**: Which pressures have a near-term consequence if not addressed?
**High urgency / Lower stakes**: Which pressures feel urgent but are actually manageable with a clear response?
**Lower urgency / High stakes**: Which pressures are slow-building but will matter significantly in 12-24 months?
**Background noise**: Which pressures are real but don't require action — they require monitoring?

---

## Step 4: Identify the Real Ask

For each high-urgency pressure, surface the actual ask — what would a satisfactory response look like?

Often the real ask is smaller than it appears:
- A board mandate often requires a *narrative*, not a *strategy*
- A competitive signal often requires *analysis*, not *response*
- A talent signal often requires *one visible tool*, not an *AI programme*

The discipline here is resisting the pressure to respond at the wrong level. Treating every AI pressure as a strategy problem leads to over-investment in strategy and under-investment in the specific things that would actually satisfy the actual ask.

---

## Output

Produce a one-page pressure map:

1. **Inventory**: What you're hearing, from whom
2. **Categorisation**: Type for each item
3. **Priority map**: Urgency / stakes grid
4. **Real asks**: What each high-priority pressure actually requires
5. **Recommended sequence**: Which to address first, in what order, with what type of response

---

## Principle

*Most AI adoption pressure is not pressure to deploy AI. It is pressure to demonstrate that you understand the moment. Those are different problems with different solutions.*

Name the pressure precisely. Then respond to the thing you named.
