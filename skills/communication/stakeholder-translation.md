# Stakeholder Translation

The same AI position, communicated in the wrong register to the wrong audience, either fails to land or actively damages trust. A board needs a different cut of the same reality than a frontline team. An investor conversation requires a different frame than a regulator meeting. A client briefing carries different risks than an employee town hall.

Stakeholder translation is not spin — it is not changing the substance to suit the audience. It is identifying what each audience is actually trying to understand, what their specific concerns are, what they have authority over, and what register of communication earns their trust.

Start with the same ground truth for every audience. Change the frame, the emphasis, and the level of detail. Never change the underlying position.

---

## Step 1: Establish the Ground Truth

Before translating for any audience, articulate your actual AI position in plain language — for yourself, not for any audience.

Answer:

- What are you actually doing on AI right now?
- What have you decided not to do, and why?
- What are you uncertain about and watching?
- What is the honest timeline on value delivery?
- What are the real risks you're managing?

This is the document you don't show anyone. Every audience translation should be derivable from it without contradicting it. If two audience versions would contradict each other if placed side by side, the ground truth is not yet honest enough.

---

## Step 2: Identify the Audience

For each translation, specify:

- Who specifically is in the room or reading the communication?
- What do they already know about your AI position?
- What are they trying to decide, assess, or understand?
- What is their primary concern — the thing that, if not addressed, will make everything else not land?
- What is their relationship to risk — are they responsible for managing it, bearing it, or approving it?
- What would a successful communication look like from their perspective?

## Step 3: Apply the Translation Template

### Board of Directors

**What they're trying to understand**: Whether executive leadership has a coherent, calibrated AI position — and whether it represents the right level of ambition and caution for the organisation.

**Primary concern**: Strategic risk and reputational exposure. They are not evaluating the technology; they are evaluating the judgment of the people running the organisation.

**What earns their trust**: Specificity, honest acknowledgement of uncertainty, and evidence that you have thought about the downside scenarios not just the upside. A board that hears an AI narrative with no failure modes mentioned will distrust it. A board that hears one with explicit failure modes and management responses will trust the executive more.

**What to emphasise**: Your position and the reasoning behind it. What you're doing, what you've decided against, what you're watching. The governance you have in place.

**What to minimise**: Technical detail. Vendor names. Pilot-level outcomes presented as strategic wins.

**Register**: Measured, direct, willing to acknowledge uncertainty. The board has been in rooms with a lot of AI confidence. Calibrated caution reads as mature judgment.

---

### Investors (Public Company / PE / Venture-backed)

**What they're trying to understand**: Whether AI represents a value creation opportunity, a competitive risk, or both — and whether management is navigating it competently.

**Primary concern**: Relative competitive position and capital allocation. They want to know whether you're ahead, behind, or appropriately cautious — and whether the investment thesis is intact.

**What earns their trust**: A clear narrative with specific evidence. Investors discount assertions; they weight specifics. ROI figures tied to real deployments, named capability investments with a clear logic, honest competitor benchmarking.

**What to emphasise**: The strategic rationale for your AI investments. How AI connects to the core value drivers of the business. The specific capabilities you're building or buying and why they matter competitively.

**What to minimise**: Generic statements about AI's importance. Initiatives that are not yet producing measurable results, unless framed carefully as deliberate learning investments.

**Register**: Confident on the strategic direction, honest about the operational stage. If you're early, say so and explain what early-stage learning looks like for you. If you're seeing results, be specific.

---

### Regulators

**What they're trying to understand**: Whether your AI use is within a risk framework they can evaluate — and whether you are a trustworthy actor in a domain they are still developing oversight tools for.

**Primary concern**: Compliance, risk controls, and evidence that you have thought seriously about failure modes. Regulators are not trying to slow you down — they are trying to confirm that you understand the risks of what you're doing.

**What earns their trust**: Governance specificity. A clear account of what AI is being used for, what data is involved, who is responsible for each deployment, what the human oversight mechanisms are, and what happens when it fails. Evidence that these frameworks existed before the regulator asked, not in response to the question.

**What to emphasise**: Your governance architecture. How AI outputs are reviewed before they are used in consequential decisions. Your approach to failure mode identification and response.

**What to minimise**: Strategic ambition. Competitive claims. Efficiency gains that imply reduced human oversight. Anything that sounds like the goal is speed.

**Register**: Methodical, transparent, and oriented toward accountability. The regulator should leave the conversation believing you have thought harder about the risks than they have prompted you to.

---

### Clients and Customers

**What they're trying to understand**: Whether AI changes what they're getting from you — and whether it changes it in ways that affect their trust in you.

**Primary concern**: Quality, reliability, and whether their data or their relationship with you is being changed without their knowledge or consent.

**What earns their trust**: Clarity about what AI is and isn't doing in the work you deliver for them. Specificity about how AI output is reviewed before it reaches them. An honest answer to "is my data being used to train AI systems?"

**What to emphasise**: What isn't changing — the human judgment, the accountability, the quality standards they're used to. And what is changing — and why it benefits them, with specific evidence rather than general assertion.

**What to minimise**: AI as a cost efficiency story. Capability claims that raise expectations you haven't yet met. Technical detail that is not relevant to the client relationship.

**Register**: Reassuring on the relationship, honest on the change. Clients who sense they are not being told the full story will draw their own conclusions. The full story, told well, is almost always better than the managed version.

---

### Employees

**What they're trying to understand**: What AI means for their jobs — specifically, whether their role is changing, whether their expertise is being devalued, and whether they should be worried.

**Primary concern**: Job security, professional identity, and whether they are being asked to participate in their own replacement.

**What earns their trust**: Specificity, honesty, and evidence that you have thought about the human impact of the change — not just the operational benefit. Generic reassurance ("AI will create new opportunities") is not believed. Specific, honest accounts of what is changing, for whom, on what timeline, are.

**What to emphasise**: What you know specifically about how the change will affect their roles. What support is available during the transition. What you don't know yet and how you'll communicate as you find out.

**What to minimise**: Strategic framing. ROI language. Competitor benchmarking. Anything that sounds like the goal is to do more with fewer people, even if it's true.

**Register**: Human, direct, and honest about uncertainty. The worst employee communication about AI is confident and vague. The best is uncertain and specific — because employees can tell the difference, and they trust the honest version.

---

### Media

**What they're trying to understand**: The story — either that you're doing something interesting, doing something risky, or saying something that contradicts what another executive in your industry has said.

**Primary concern**: A quote that is crisp, quotable, and holds up under scrutiny. They are not trying to embarrass you; they are trying to write something worth reading.

**What earns their trust**: A position, stated clearly, that doesn't backpedal when followed up. Specificity that gives them something concrete to write about. Honest acknowledgement of what you don't know.

**What to avoid**: Generic statements that could be said by any executive. Claims that cannot be supported by evidence you can provide. Any assertion that you would not want to see as a headline.

**Register**: Crisp. One clear position, well-stated, not over-qualified. Media translate executive hedge into executive uncertainty; media translate executive specificity into executive credibility.

## Principle

**The translation is in the frame, not the facts.** Every audience gets the same underlying reality. What changes is which aspect of that reality is foregrounded, which level of detail is appropriate, and which register produces the trust required for the communication to do its job.

If you find yourself changing the facts rather than the frame, the ground truth isn't honest enough. Go back to Step 1.
