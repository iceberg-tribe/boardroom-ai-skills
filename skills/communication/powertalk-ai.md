# Powertalk on AI

There are two ways to talk about AI in a senior room.

The first is **posture talk**: the language of strategy decks, press releases, and peer benchmarking. It signals awareness of the trend, alignment with the moment, and absence of obvious risk. It is designed to produce no objection. It rarely produces any value either — because everyone in the room is doing the same thing, and everyone knows it.

The second is **power talk**: the language of actual stakes. It surfaces real constraints, names genuine uncertainty, takes positions that can be held under questioning, and treats the people in the room as capable of engaging with complexity. It is rarer, harder, and — when done well — the only communication that earns real credibility.

Most executive AI communication is posture talk dressed as substance. This skill is about the other kind.

---

## The Diagnostic

Before working on what to say, identify which mode you've been operating in.

**Signs you've been in posture mode:**

- You've described your AI position in terms that would apply to any organisation in your sector
- You've cited industry figures or analyst reports rather than your own operational experience
- You've answered AI questions with the answer you thought the room wanted rather than the one you believed
- You've avoided saying "I don't know" by saying something that means "I don't know" without admitting it
- You've felt relieved when an AI conversation ended without a hard follow-up

**Signs you've been in power mode:**

- You've described what you're actually doing, including what isn't working
- You've named the specific uncertainty you're operating under
- You've taken a position that could be challenged — and held it when it was
- You've said "I don't know, and here's how I'm finding out" without feeling it was a concession
- The people in the room left knowing something specific about your situation, not just your orientation

The goal is not to eliminate posture — posture has a function in professional settings. The goal is to know when you're in posture mode and be able to choose power mode when it matters.

## The Power Talk Moves

These are the specific moves that shift AI conversation from posture to substance. Each one is a sentence structure you can use.

### Move 1: The Honest Anchor

Instead of opening with your position, open with the constraint that shapes it.

**Posture version**: "We're committed to AI adoption and are investing across multiple fronts."

**Power version**: "The honest constraint we're operating under is [data maturity / talent availability / regulatory uncertainty / capital allocation priority]. That's what's shaping our pace, and here's why we think it's the right pace given that."

The honest anchor works because it demonstrates that your position is the output of thinking, not the default. It also pre-empts the hardest follow-up question by naming the constraint yourself.

### Move 2: The Specific Claim

Replace general assertions with specific claims that could, in principle, be tested.

**Posture version**: "We're seeing real productivity gains from our AI investments."

**Power version**: "In [specific function], we've reduced [specific task] time by approximately [specific figure] — though I want to be clear this is a pilot result and we haven't confirmed it scales."

The specific claim is more credible than the general assertion even when the numbers are smaller. A room full of executives has heard enough AI success stories to discount unqualified assertions. Specificity, including honest qualification of what you don't yet know, reads as the real thing.

### Move 3: The Named Uncertainty

Proactively name the two or three things you don't know and are watching.

**Posture version**: "Like everyone, we're monitoring the space closely."

**Power version**: "The two things I'm watching most closely are [first specific signal] and [second specific signal] — because they would each change our approach materially. The first because [reason]. The second because [reason]."

Named uncertainty is not weakness. It is the signal that your current position is based on a considered view under genuine uncertainty — which is the only intellectually honest position available in AI right now. Anyone claiming certainty in this space is either very far ahead or performing confidence.

### Move 4: The Calibrated Disagreement

When you disagree with a claim being made in the room — a vendor assertion, a peer's stated position, an analyst's forecast — name the disagreement specifically rather than absorbing it.

**Posture version**: *[Nods, asks a clarifying question, moves on]*

**Power version**: "I want to push back on one thing. [Specific claim] assumes [specific condition], and my experience is that [specific counter-evidence or reason for doubt]. I could be wrong about this — but I think it's worth holding the question open."

The calibrated disagreement is the most powerful move in a senior room because it is so rarely made. Executives who push back on claims with specific reasoning — rather than general skepticism or passive absorption — are the ones whose judgment is trusted. The qualification ("I could be wrong") is not hedging. It is the marker of a serious position, held with appropriate confidence.

### Move 5: The Honest I Don't Know

When you don't know something, say so — with what you're doing about it.

**Posture version**: "It's a complex area and we're looking at it carefully."

**Power version**: "I genuinely don't know the answer to that. What I'm doing to find out is [specific action]. I'll have a clearer view by [specific timeframe]."

The honest "I don't know" followed by a specific path to knowing is more credible than any confident answer that the room suspects is incomplete. It is also the move that most consistently surprises rooms that have been in posture mode — because it signals that the person speaking is operating in reality rather than managing impressions.

## Preparing for the Hard Questions

Before any high-stakes AI conversation, prepare for these. Not with scripted answers — with a genuine position on each.

**"Are we moving fast enough?"**
Your answer should be: here is our pace, here is why it's the right pace given [constraint], here is what would cause me to change it.

**"What are our competitors doing?"**
Your answer should be: here is what I know specifically, here is what I'm uncertain about, here is why I think the competitive picture is [more / less / differently] threatening than it appears.

**"What's our AI strategy?"**
Your answer should be: here is what we're doing, here is what we've decided not to do, here is what we're watching before we decide on the rest. If you don't have a position on all three, prepare the one you have and be honest about the ones you don't.

**"What's the risk?"**
Your answer should be: here are the specific risks in our sector and organisation — not generic AI risks — and here is how we're managing the ones that matter most. If you name the risks before being asked to, you control the framing.

**A question you can't answer:**
Your answer should be: "I don't know. Let me tell you what I do know, and I'll follow up on the rest." Never fill a gap with confident vagueness. The room will remember the gap; they will respect the honesty.

---

## The Register

Power talk on AI does not sound like confidence for its own sake. It sounds like a person who has thought hard about a genuinely uncertain situation and arrived at calibrated positions they can defend.

The texture of the register:

- Specific over general
- Qualified where appropriate, unqualified where not
- Willing to name what you don't know
- Willing to push back on what you disagree with
- Grounded in your own operational experience rather than borrowed authority

The goal is not to sound smart about AI. It is to be useful to the room — which means bringing your actual knowledge, actual uncertainty, and actual judgment, rather than a curated version of all three.

That is the harder thing. It is also the only thing that builds lasting credibility.
