# AI Readiness Read

Your organisation's stated AI readiness and its actual AI readiness are two different numbers.

The gap between them is structural, not deceptive. Teams under pressure to show progress have every incentive to present optimistic pictures. Tools adoption is visible; workflow change is not. Pilots get announced; quiet failures get absorbed. The executive at the top of this information structure receives a systematically distorted picture.

This skill helps you structure a more accurate read — not by auditing your teams, but by knowing which signals to look for and which questions to ask.

---

## Step 1: Define What Readiness Means for Your Organisation

AI readiness is not a universal concept. Before you can assess it, you need to define what it means in your context.

Answer these three questions:

**What would a genuinely AI-ready team be able to do that they can't do now?**
Be specific. "Use AI tools effectively" is not an answer. "Reduce client proposal preparation time by 40% using AI-assisted research and drafting, with human review of all outputs before delivery" is an answer.

**What is the minimum viable AI capability your organisation needs in the next 12 months to remain competitive / compliant / efficient?**
This anchors the assessment to something consequential. Without this, "readiness" becomes a floating standard.

**What would a failure of AI readiness look like?**
The clearest signal of underpreparedness is often visible only in retrospect. Try to name it in advance: losing a contract to a competitor with better AI-assisted delivery, a compliance incident caused by ungoverned AI use, key talent leaving for organisations with better AI tooling.

## Step 2: Map the Readiness Landscape

For each significant function or team, assess across four dimensions. This is a structured conversation — gather input from your direct reports, but also from one level below, and from any function heads you trust to be candid.

### Dimension 1: Tool Adoption vs Workflow Integration

*Tool adoption*: People have access to AI tools and use them occasionally.
*Workflow integration*: AI is embedded in how the work actually gets done — it would be noticed if it disappeared.

**Signal questions**:

- Which AI tools are in active daily use, and for what specific tasks?
- If those tools were removed tomorrow, would output quality or speed change? By how much?
- Are there teams where AI has changed what work they do, not just how they do it?

*The gap to watch*: Teams that report high AI adoption but cannot name specific workflow changes. Tools adoption without workflow integration is capability theatre.

### Dimension 2: Data Readiness

Most AI capability depends on data — its availability, quality, and governance. Data readiness is the most consistently underestimated dimension and the hardest to retrofit.

**Signal questions**:

- Which teams have structured, clean, accessible data relevant to their core work?
- Where is data siloed, inconsistent, or ungoverned?
- Which teams would hit a data wall within the first month of a serious AI deployment?

*The gap to watch*: Teams enthusiastic about AI use cases who have not yet encountered their own data constraints. The enthusiasm will survive until the first real deployment attempt.

### Dimension 3: Talent Distribution

AI readiness is not evenly distributed. Typically: a small number of individuals are genuinely capable and driving most of the real work; a larger group is learning; a significant portion is performing readiness without underlying change. Misreading this distribution leads to overconfident deployment timelines.

**Signal questions**:

- Who, specifically, is doing real AI work? Can you name them?
- What would happen to the team's AI capability if those individuals left?
- Where is AI enthusiasm driven by individual initiative vs organisational infrastructure?
- Which managers understand AI well enough to coach their teams, vs which are relying entirely on their team to figure it out?

*The gap to watch*: AI capability concentrated in 1-2 individuals in a function, presented as team-level readiness.

### Dimension 4: Governance and Risk Awareness

Ungoverned AI use is a risk that often only becomes visible at the point of a failure. This dimension assesses whether your organisation's AI use is within a risk framework or outside one.

**Signal questions**:

- Do your teams know which AI tools are approved and which are not?
- Is there a policy on what data can be input into external AI systems?
- Has anyone in your organisation produced an AI output that was used in a client-facing or regulated context without human review?
- Do your teams know what to do when an AI output is wrong in a consequential way?

*The gap to watch*: Teams who are moving quickly on AI adoption without having thought about failure modes. Speed here is not a readiness signal — it may be a risk signal.

## Step 3: Identify the Genuine Capability Pockets

Based on the landscape, identify the functions or teams where genuine AI readiness exists — where adoption is real, workflow integration is visible, data is workable, and risk awareness is present.

These are your internal centres of gravity. They are worth:

- Investing in further, so they become more capable and more replicable
- Documenting, so you understand what made them ready (was it leadership? data advantage? team composition? external pressure?)
- Using as internal references, so other teams can learn from actual deployment experience rather than vendor demos

---

## Step 4: Identify the Priority Gaps

Of the gaps identified, which matter most — given your minimum viable AI capability definition from Step 1?

Not all gaps require immediate attention. Some functions may be appropriately slow. The assessment should produce a prioritised view: where does the gap between stated readiness and actual readiness create the most consequential risk?

Common high-priority gap patterns:

- **The governance gap**: Rapid, ungoverned AI adoption in a regulated function
- **The key-person gap**: AI capability that lives in one person and will leave with them
- **The data gap that's blocking the highest-value use case**: Where the most anticipated AI deployment is stalled by data infrastructure nobody has fixed
- **The leadership gap**: A function where the manager cannot evaluate AI output quality, creating unreviewed AI in client or operational workflows

---

## Step 5: Produce the Read

Produce a structured summary for your own use:

**The honest assessment** (2-3 sentences): What is the real state of AI readiness in your organisation, stated plainly?

**The genuine capability pockets**: Named teams or functions, with what they're actually doing.

**The priority gaps**: The two or three gaps with the highest consequence, with a specific owner who should be addressing each.

**The one thing that, if fixed, would most improve your organisation's AI position in the next 6 months**: One specific thing — a data infrastructure investment, a governance policy, a key hire, a pilot in a specific function. Focus the constraint.

## Principles

**Stated readiness is a lagging indicator.** By the time a team reports readiness to you, the picture has already been curated. The leading indicators are in the specifics: names, workflows, data, failure modes.

**Concentration is fragile.** AI capability concentrated in individuals — however talented — is not organisational readiness. It is personal readiness that happens to be in your organisation temporarily.

**The information structure is biased upward.** Every level of your organisation has an incentive to report progress on AI. This is not dishonesty — it is the normal operation of performance cultures under uncertainty. Build in sources that are not subject to that bias: direct conversations with individual contributors, observation of actual workflows, the questions teams are asking rather than the answers they're giving.

**Readiness is always relative to a specific use case.** "Are we ready for AI?" is not a useful question. "Are we ready to deploy AI-assisted contract review in our legal function in Q3?" is. The specificity of the use case determines what readiness means and whether you have it.
