# CONTEXT.md — Shared Vocabulary

This file defines the language used across all skills in this repo. When in doubt, use these terms precisely. Imprecise language in this domain causes imprecise thinking.

---

## Core Terms

**AI Theatre**
Demonstrations of AI capability that are optimised for impressiveness rather than operational validity. AI theatre is not always dishonest — often it reflects genuine optimism about deployment timelines and integration complexity. The distinguishing feature: it is built on curated conditions that do not reflect the environment in which it will actually need to perform.

**AI Substance**
AI deployment that has survived contact with real operations: messy data, resistant workflows, underskilled users, and the ordinary friction of organisational change. Substance is distinguished not by the quality of the demo but by the existence of a named process owner, a failure mode analysis, and a recovery protocol.

**Adoption Pressure**
The external force on an executive to demonstrate AI engagement — from board mandates, competitive signalling, market narrative, or internal advocacy. Adoption pressure is not the same as adoption rationale. Many executives are under high adoption pressure with low adoption rationale, and confusing the two is a primary source of poor AI investment decisions.

**The Translation Gap**
The distance between what an AI vendor claims and what your organisation can actually execute. The translation gap is not primarily a technology problem. It is a capability, culture, and data problem. Most AI vendors price as if the gap is small. Most organisations discover it is large.

**Epistemic Navigation**
The executive's actual problem in the AI moment: not knowing what to believe, in a domain where almost every person with information also has an incentive to present it selectively. Epistemic navigation is the skill of forming calibrated beliefs under motivated information asymmetry.

**The Briefing Problem**
The structural difficulty of getting accurate AI information from people who want to impress you, protect their turf, or avoid accountability for past positions. The briefing problem means that an executive's internal AI picture is systematically biased toward optimism at the top and toward defensiveness at the functional level.

**Adoption Posture vs Adoption Stakes**
*Posture*: the public position an executive holds on AI — the stated strategy, the announced initiatives, the board-level narrative.
*Stakes*: what the executive actually needs AI to do, by when, at what cost, to avoid what outcome.
Most AI conversations operate at the level of posture. The skills in this repo operate at the level of stakes.

**The Commitment Horizon**
The point at which an executive's AI position becomes binding — when a vendor contract is signed, a board commitment is made, a budget is allocated, or an organisational change is announced. Everything before the commitment horizon is optionality. Everything after is cost. A key executive skill is knowing where the horizon is before crossing it.

**Capability Theatre**
A specific form of AI theatre internal to organisations: teams demonstrating AI readiness through tools adoption, training completions, and pilot announcements — without underlying workflow change. Capability theatre is particularly hard to detect because it uses all the right metrics.

**The Pilot Trap**
The pattern in which AI pilots succeed on their own terms but fail to scale — because the conditions that made the pilot work (dedicated team, clean data, motivated users, executive attention) do not generalise to the rest of the organisation. The pilot trap creates false confidence and delayed reckoning.

## Diagnostic Terms

These terms are used specifically in the diagnostic skills (ai-theatre-detector, vendor-interrogation, ai-readiness-read).

**Production Validity**
Whether a claimed AI capability has been tested against production-quality inputs: real data, real volume, real edge cases, real failure modes. A capability without production validity is a demo.

**Process Owner**
The named individual responsible for a specific AI-integrated workflow — including when it fails. The absence of a named process owner is a reliable signal of AI theatre.

**Failure Mode Acknowledgement**
Whether a vendor, team, or proposal has explicitly described the conditions under which the AI system produces wrong, harmful, or null output — and what the response protocol is. The absence of failure mode acknowledgement is a red flag.

**Integration Depth**
The degree to which an AI capability is embedded in an existing workflow, as opposed to sitting alongside it as an optional tool. Deep integration changes the workflow. Shallow integration adds a step.

**Adoption Surface**
The number and type of people who need to change their behaviour for an AI deployment to deliver its claimed value. Large adoption surfaces require change management, not just technology deployment.
