# LinkedIn Posting Strategy — Boardroom AI Skills

**Goal:** Build a personal audience of senior executives and decision-makers who associate your name with a specific, distinctive voice on AI — calibrated, anti-hype, epistemically honest.

**Cadence:** 2–3 posts/week

---

## The Core Positioning

Most LinkedIn AI content is one of three things: hype ("AI will change everything"), anxiety ("AI will take your job"), or generic literacy ("here are five things every executive should know about AI").

Your lane is none of those. It is the lane that almost nobody occupies: *epistemic clarity for people who are already smart but surrounded by motivated information*.

The positioning, in one sentence:
> **You build the layer below the demos. You write for the people above them.**

This is your credibility anchor. You are not a consultant, not a vendor, not an AI literacy educator. You are someone who has actually built AI infrastructure and therefore knows exactly where the gap between the pitch and the operational reality lives — and you're willing to say so precisely.

That specificity is the differentiator. Protect it.

---

## The Audience

**Primary:** Senior executives (C-suite, VPs, board directors) who are under pressure to have an AI position but feel that most of what they're being told is optimised for someone else's interest.

**Secondary:** Their advisors — consultants, investors, non-executive directors — who work in the same epistemic fog and find your clarity useful as a reference point.

**Not the audience:** AI builders, ML engineers, developers. They may follow you, but writing for them will dilute the positioning. When the content addresses someone, address the person in the boardroom, not the person in the terminal.

---

## The Voice

The repo has a distinctive register. The LinkedIn content should inherit it, not water it down.

**What the voice is:**
- Declarative. Short sentences with substance. No filler.
- Specific over general. Numbers with denominators. Claims that could be challenged.
- Willing to name the uncomfortable thing. The vendor's incentive. The board's misread. The consultant's framework that doesn't fit.
- Anti-posture. Uses the word "posture" the way others use "BS" — precisely and without apology.
- Dry. Not humourless, but the wit is in the precision, not in jokes.

**What the voice is not:**
- Confident in a vague way. ("AI is transforming everything.")
- Self-promotional in an obvious way. ("Excited to share that...")
- Hedged into meaninglessness. ("It depends, of course...")
- Listicle-brained. (LinkedIn's default mode — avoid.)

**The recurring vocabulary** — these terms do real work. Use them consistently so they become associated with you:
- *AI theatre* — the gap between demo and deployment
- *Epistemic navigation* — the executive's real AI problem
- *Posture vs. stakes* — performing readiness vs. knowing what you need
- *The pilot trap* — pilots that succeed but don't scale
- *The translation gap* — the gap between vendor claim and your operational reality
- *The briefing problem* — why the information you receive is systematically distorted upward
- *Adoption pressure vs. adoption rationale* — the two things executives confuse constantly
- *Production validity* — whether a capability has survived contact with real data

Do not explain these terms every time. Use them as if they're already in the reader's vocabulary. Over time, they will be.

## Content Pillars

Five pillars, each mapping to a real cluster of executive pain.

### Pillar 1: AI Theatre (The Detector)
*Posts that help executives recognize performance vs. substance*

This is the hook pillar — the highest shareability, because every executive has been in a vendor meeting they didn't trust. The "AI Theatre Detector" and "Vendor Interrogation" skills are the source material.

Representative post angles:
- The one signal that a vendor demo is theatre (no failure mode disclosure)
- What "it's continuously improving" actually means when a vendor says it
- The question to ask at the start of every AI vendor meeting (before they show you anything)
- Why your seniority is a liability in the vendor room — they're optimising for you specifically
- ROI figures with no denominator: what to do with them

### Pillar 2: The Briefing Problem (Epistemic)
*Posts about the structural distortion of information executives receive*

The most philosophically distinctive pillar — the one that names something executives feel but rarely see articulated. This is where the term *epistemic navigation* earns its weight.

Representative post angles:
- Why every level of your organisation has an incentive to report AI progress that isn't there
- The gap between stated AI readiness and actual AI readiness (why it's structural, not deceptive)
- What "our team is exploring AI" almost always means in practice
- The difference between the question you asked and the question you got answered
- How to read an internal AI briefing when you know it's biased

### Pillar 3: Pressure Analysis (The Strategist)
*Posts that help executives disaggregate the different kinds of AI pressure they're under*

Most executives are responding to five different kinds of AI pressure as if they were one. Naming them separately is a genuine service. The "Name the Pressure" skill is the source material.

Representative post angles:
- A board asking about AI is not asking for a deployment. What it's actually asking for.
- The six types of AI pressure executives face — and why they require completely different responses
- Competitive AI announcements: what to read, what to discount
- When "we need an AI strategy" is actually a governance problem in disguise
- Talent pressure vs. AI strategy — why confusing them is expensive

### Pillar 4: Organisational Reality (The Read)
*Posts about the gap between what organisations say they can do and what they can actually do*

The *pilot trap*, *capability theatre*, and readiness assessment material lives here. High resonance with executives who have been burned by scaling failures.

Representative post angles:
- The pilot trap: why AI pilots succeed and deployments fail (the conditions don't generalise)
- Capability theatre: what it looks like when teams perform readiness rather than build it
- AI capability concentrated in one person is not organisational readiness
- The middle layer problem: why AI change programmes stall at the manager level
- What "resistance to AI" is usually actually about (and why it often carries accurate signal)

### Pillar 5: Honest Communication (The Register)
*Posts about how to talk about AI without performing*

The *Powertalk* skill is the source material. This pillar resonates because it gives executives permission to be honest in rooms full of posture.

Representative post angles:
- The difference between posture talk and power talk in AI conversations
- How to say "I don't know" in a way that builds credibility rather than losing it
- Why the executive who pushes back with specific reasoning is the one the room trusts
- The board AI narrative: what boards actually want to hear vs. what they get
- Talking to employees about AI without generic reassurance

## Post Formats

LinkedIn rewards specific formats. Use these in rotation.

### The Named Observation (2–5 paragraphs)
A specific observation about how AI decisions actually go wrong — named precisely, explained briefly. No lists. No headers. Just the observation.

*This is the pillar format — the one that builds the distinctive voice fastest.*

Example structure:
- Para 1: Name the pattern. ("There is a specific kind of AI demo that passes every test in the room and fails every test in production.")
- Para 2: Describe it precisely. What it looks like. Why it happens.
- Para 3: The signal. What gives it away.
- Para 4 (optional): What to do instead, or a question to sit with.

Length: 150–250 words.

### The Reframe (1 sentence + 3–5 paragraphs)
Opens with a statement that contradicts the LinkedIn consensus on AI, then explains why. Designed for engagement from people who disagree — and agreement from people who already felt this but hadn't named it.

Example openers:
- "Most AI adoption pressure is not pressure to deploy AI."
- "The pilot worked. That's the problem."
- "Your board's AI question is not an AI question."
- "The executive in the room with the most AI confidence is usually the least useful one to listen to."

### The Tool Drop (1–2 paragraphs + link)
Direct, low-friction post that introduces one specific skill from the repo. No preamble, no hype. Describe the problem it solves, describe who it's for, link to the repo.

These should be occasional — when a topic is trending (e.g., a major AI announcement creates a wave of vendor activity), a tool drop is timely and useful.

### The Diagnostic Question (1 short paragraph)
A question that functions as a self-assessment. No answer provided in the post — the question is the content. Draws comments from people working through their answer.

Examples:
- "If you removed the two people most responsible for your organisation's AI work, how much of the capability would leave with them?"
- "When was the last time a vendor described a failure mode before you asked?"
- "What is your 'not doing' list on AI? Not 'haven't got to yet' — explicitly decided against, with a reason."

## Growth Mechanics

### What drives following on LinkedIn with this audience

Senior executives do not follow content that feels like content. They follow people who:
- Say something they already believed but hadn't named
- Disagree with something confidently and turn out to be right
- Give them a tool that worked in the room

Design every post to do at least one of these things.

### Engagement strategy

**Comment selectively on others' posts.** Find the big AI hype posts from consultants, analysts, and vendors — and comment with a specific, calibrated counter-observation. Not dismissiveness; precision. A comment that demonstrates your voice will send more profile views than a post.

**Don't ask for engagement.** No "What do you think?" No "Drop a comment below." The audience you're building finds this register grating. Let the content invite conversation by being specific enough to provoke a response.

**Connect after a strong comment.** When someone engages thoughtfully with a post — not just "great point!" but an actual reaction — send a connection request with a one-sentence note about what they said.

### Amplification

**Tag sparingly.** Tagging other people in posts is high-noise, low-signal. Only tag when their work is directly relevant and you'd genuinely recommend it.

**Share to other channels when you have them.** The repo is a natural Hacker News post ("Ask HN: I built prompt skills for executives navigating AI adoption pressure"). The same community that reads the repo will also read the LinkedIn posts.

**Cross-post the tool drops.** When you publish a tool drop post, that's also the right moment to link the post in any community where the repo has already gotten traction.

## What to Avoid

**Avoid the LinkedIn publishing conventions that signal generic content:**
- "I'm excited to share..."
- "Let me know your thoughts in the comments!"
- "This is why [trending AI thing] changes everything"
- Headers and bullet lists in every post (use sparingly; the named observation format is prose)
- Carousel posts (too much effort for this audience; they'll read prose)
- Personal milestone posts ("Thrilled to announce...")

**Avoid false modesty.**
"I'm just a builder who..." undercuts the positioning. You know what you know. The voice is confident and specific, not hedged with credibility caveats.

**Avoid generic AI literacy content.**
"Here's what LLMs actually are" or "5 ways executives can use ChatGPT" — this content has infinite supply. Your lane is the opposite: not what AI is, but what to believe about it and who to trust.

**Avoid fighting the hype directly.**
Don't position the account as "the skeptic." The position is not skepticism; it is calibration. Some AI capabilities are genuine; the discipline is knowing which ones on what evidence. Anti-hype for its own sake is as unsophisticated as hype.

## Horizon: What a Successful Account Looks Like at 6 Months

By six months of consistent posting at 2–3x/week, the account should:

- Have established recognition for the specific vocabulary (people will use "AI theatre" in comments)
- Be receiving direct messages from executives who want to talk through a specific vendor situation or board meeting
- Have driven enough traffic to the repo that the "Let's talk" link is receiving occasional inbound requests
- Have been shared by at least a few advisors/consultants who use the skills with clients

The measure of success is not follower count. It is whether the people in the room you're writing for are quoting your vocabulary.
